I learned to code because I wanted to build things. That used to be a simple sentence to act on. Lately it isn't, and I don't think that's a bad sign. It means I'm finally asking the right question: if AI can generate code faster than I can type it, what am I actually getting paid for?

I came to engineering from journalism. I spent years asking "what's the real story here" before "what's the right data structure here." So when AI started doing a lot of my typing, the instinct that kicked in wasn't panic. It was the older one: *don't print what you haven't verified.*

## The gap I actually live in

Stack Overflow's 2025 Developer Survey found that 84% of developers now use or plan to use AI tools. That's the floor, not the ceiling. But trust didn't follow the adoption curve — only 33% said they trust AI's accuracy, 46% actively distrust it, and just 3% say they "highly trust" the output. Stack Overflow's own CEO summed up why: "AI is a powerful tool, but it has significant risks of misinformation."

Adoption up, trust down. That gap is where my job lives now.

The career data says the same thing from a different angle. PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer found entry-level roles exposed to AI are seven times more likely to demand senior skills — judgment, leadership, stakeholder communication — than less-exposed roles. PwC's global workforce leader named the mechanism plainly: "AI is removing some of the routine work that once acted as an apprenticeship."

The easy reps — the ones I used to learn the job *through* — are the first thing AI eats. What's left to prove was never about typing speed.

## What changed for me

I used to think building meant opening the editor and making files. Idea → data model → routes → components. Not wrong, just incomplete — and AI makes the gap worse, not better, because it'll happily hand you a confident, fully-typed answer to a question you hadn't finished asking.

Now, three non-negotiables before I touch a file:

**Non-goals before goals.** Not what this should do — what I'm intentionally not solving yet. A promise to my future self that scope won't creep while I'm not looking.

**The riskiest assumption first.** Not the easiest task. What's most likely wrong about the user, the data, the tech — that's where projects actually die.

**AI output gets treated like an anonymous tip.** Useful, sometimes exactly right, never run without a second source. If I can't explain why generated code works, I haven't verified it — I've just trusted it.

None of this slows me down. It's where the speed comes from — I stop re-architecting three days in after building the wrong thing well.

## The bar didn't lower. It moved.

I still respect rigor — clean systems, tested code, scalable architecture. AI doesn't get to be an excuse for skipping that. But it's not the whole bar anymore. Companies are adopting AI fast and trusting it slowly. The market is asking junior engineers to bring judgment that used to take a decade to earn.

I can't out-type a model. I never could. What I can do is decide what's worth building, catch the thing that's almost right but not quite, and explain why a decision was made — to a teammate, a user, myself.

I was taught to build. Now I'm learning what's worth building in the first place.
